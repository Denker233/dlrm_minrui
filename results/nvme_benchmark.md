## 5. Answers to the Instruction Questions

**Does any QD reach < 10 ms (SLA target)?** Yes — 9.3 ms at QD>=32, single-threaded.
Marginal: it consumes the entire 10 ms budget on I/O alone, leaving nothing for compute.

**Does any QD reach < 5 ms (DRAM forward time)?** No, not single-threaded. The device
ceiling of 504k IOPS implies 1,660 reads could complete in ~3.3 ms, but only with ~4
cores dedicated to I/O completion.

**At what QD does NVMe saturate?** QD=32 for a single thread (CPU-limited at ~180k IOPS).
The device itself needs 4 concurrent jobs to saturate at ~504k IOPS.

**Best case for our access pattern?** 9.3 ms single-threaded; ~3.3 ms extrapolated with 4
I/O cores. Against DC block-mean's **0 ms** and a 4.40 ms full forward pass, even the
optimistic figure is I/O time added *before* any compute.

**Cost-effectiveness.** NVMe at $100-300/TB vs the DRAM that DC compression frees
(2 GB -> 11 MB on Kaggle) — but the comparison is not storage cost alone. Serving from
NVMe at the ceiling requires ~4 cores of completion processing per serving instance,
and still adds 3-9 ms to a 4.4 ms forward pass. DC removes the I/O rather than
accelerating it.

## 6. Caveats

1. **Synthetic offsets.** Generated by `generate_nvme_workload.py` — 1,660 uniformly
   random rows over 33.4M. The real measured workload has locality: 1,660 cold rows fell
   into 882 unique 4 KB pages, and the SATA page-path run reported 1,004 pages. Here the
   page path found 1,660 (no dedup possible), so **the 4 KB column is pessimistic**;
   with real offsets it would roughly halve. The 64 B row column does 1,660 reads either
   way and is directly comparable to SATA.
2. **Single-threaded.** All io_uring numbers use one thread. See section 2 for the ceiling.
3. **CPU at 1200 MHz** under `schedutil` inflates per-I/O latency; a `performance`
   governor would improve the QD=1 figures.
4. Ran concurrently with Terabyte preprocessing on the *other* NVMe. Verified isolated:
   separate device, load average 1.15, 34/503 GB RAM in use.
5. `bench_iouring.c` was patched to evict only its own file's pages via
   `posix_fadvise(DONTNEED)` instead of a global `drop_caches`; `BENCH_DROP=global`
   restores the original.

# Part 2 — Optimised NVMe Baseline

Part 1 measured NVMe the way `bench_iouring.c` was written: one thread, buffered 4 KB
reads, CPU at 1200 MHz. That is a *conservative* baseline and unfair to compare against
DC. This section is the opposite: the strongest number this hardware can produce.

## Optimisations applied (`bench_iouring_opt.c`)

| # | Change | Why |
|--:|---|---|
| 1 | CPU governor `schedutil` -> `performance` | cores were idling at 1200 MHz, now 3100 MHz |
| 2 | `O_DIRECT` + **512 B** sector reads | device LBA format is 512 B; 8x less read amplification than a 4 KB page, and no page-cache copy |
| 3 | `IORING_SETUP_IOPOLL` | busy-polled completions, no interrupt latency |
| 4 | Registered files + registered buffers | no per-op fd lookup or page pinning |
| 5 | N threads, one ring each | a single core saturates at ~520k IOPS; the device does more |
| 6 | NUMA pinning per device | `nvme0n1` is on node 0, `nvme1n1` on node 1 — threads pinned to the matching node |
| 7 | Striping across **both** NVMe drives | 2x device parallelism |
| 8 | Sector-packed cold layout | co-accessed rows share a 512 B sector, cutting I/O count |

## Results (1,660 cold rows, 200 rounds, ms)

### Threads and devices

| threads | QD | devices | min | mean | p50 | p99 | IOPS |
|--:|--:|--:|--:|--:|--:|--:|--:|
| 1 | 128 | 1 | 3.95 | 4.03 | 4.01 | 5.18 | 411,806 |
| 2 | 64 | 1 | 3.13 | 3.18 | 3.15 | 4.27 | 522,267 |
| 8 | 64 | 1 | 3.15 | 3.23 | 3.21 | 3.98 | 513,818 |
| 4 | 128 | 2 | 1.65 | 1.91 | 1.68 | 4.20 | 869,212 |
| 8 | 64 | 2 | 1.62 | 1.87 | 1.66 | 4.19 | 887,099 |
| **16** | **128** | **2** | **1.65** | **1.79** | **1.69** | **3.77** | **929,221** |

One device saturates at ~520k IOPS (~3.2 ms). Two devices reach ~930k-1.0M IOPS (~1.8 ms).
IOPOLL vs interrupt-driven made no measurable difference at these depths (1.62 vs 1.63 ms);
SQPOLL was slightly worse (1.67 ms).

### Cold-row layout locality (2 devices, 8 threads, QD=128)

`rows/sector` = how many of the 1,660 needed rows share a 512 B sector after a
locality-preserving reorder. 1 = today's scattered layout, 8 = a fully useful sector.

| rows/sector | I/Os | amplification | min | mean | p50 | p99 |
|--:|--:|--:|--:|--:|--:|--:|
| 1 | 1,660 | 8.0x | 1.61 | 1.71 | 1.66 | 3.55 |
| 2 | 830 | 4.0x | 0.85 | 0.93 | 0.90 | 1.59 |
| 4 | 415 | 2.0x | 0.47 | 0.59 | 0.56 | 2.84 |
| **8** | **207** | **1.0x** | **0.33** | **0.39** | **0.39** | **0.92** |

## The optimised baseline

| Configuration | mean | p99 |
|---|--:|--:|
| Original `bench_iouring.c` (1 thread, buffered, 1200 MHz) | 9.30 | — |
| + governor, O_DIRECT 512 B, IOPOLL, registered (1 thread) | 4.03 | 5.18 |
| + 2 threads (1 device saturated) | 3.18 | 4.27 |
| + 2nd device striped, NUMA-pinned | 1.79 | 3.77 |
| **+ sector-packed cold layout** | **0.39** | **0.92** |

**24x faster than the conservative number.**

## What this means for the DC comparison

The README currently states SSD cold serving costs 91.3 ms mean / 104.4 ms p99 and is
"infeasible for serving". That figure is a **SATA drive with serial pread**. It does not
survive contact with an optimised NVMe path, and a reviewer will find that out.

Restated honestly, at 1,660 cold rows per batch:

| Path | I/O time | Extra cores | Extra hardware | DRAM |
|---|--:|--:|---|--:|
| DRAM fp32 embedding lookup | — | 0 | — | 2,061 MB |
| DC block-mean | **0 ms** | 0 | — | **11 MB** |
| SATA SSD, serial pread | 91.3 ms | 0 | 1 SATA SSD | 0 |
| NVMe, optimised, scattered layout | 1.79 ms | 8-16 | 2 NVMe | 0 |
| NVMe, optimised, packed layout | 0.39 ms | 8 | 2 NVMe | 0 |

The optimised NVMe number is *below* the 1.46 ms DRAM embedding-lookup time. So the
honest claim is no longer "SSD is too slow". It is:

> Reaching sub-millisecond cold-embedding I/O requires two NVMe devices, 8-16 cores
> busy-polling completions, and a co-access-clustered on-disk layout. DC block-mean
> reaches 0 ms I/O with zero extra cores, one device, and 11 MB of DRAM.

## Caveats

1. **The packed-layout row (0.39 ms) is a ceiling, not a measurement of the real
   workload.** It assumes 8 co-accessed rows per sector. The original profiling found
   91.7% of touched 4 KB pages held exactly one needed row, so real cold access is much
   closer to the `rows/sector=1` row (1.79 ms). Achieving the packed layout requires the
   same access-locality reordering the DC method already uses.
2. **2015-era hardware.** Intel P3600 (SSDPEDMD020T4D). A modern PCIe Gen4 drive does
   ~1M IOPS on a *single* device at ~10-20 us latency, so one modern drive would match or
   beat this two-drive result. The baseline is hardware-limited, not protocol-limited.
3. Synthetic uniformly-random offsets, not the model's real access trace.
4. Governor left at `performance` — Part 1's numbers were taken at 1200 MHz.
